# Use event demands as a design constraint

Event-specific training does not mean swimming the event distance repeatedly every day. It means selecting velocities, skills, segment lengths, recoveries, and quality measures that transfer to the event while maintaining the athlete's broader development.

# 50 events

Common priorities:

- start-to-breakout performance;
- maximal clean swimming velocity;
- event-specific stroke rate and rhythm;
- breath or no-breath decisions practiced safely;
- finish execution;
- power and repeatable high-quality trials.

Programming guidance:

- use short, high-quality efforts with substantial recovery;
- time the same segment consistently;
- keep total maximal volume limited by quality;
- avoid converting a speed session into conditioning through rushed send-offs;
- retain aerobic and technical work elsewhere in the program.

# 100 events

Common priorities:

- high but controlled opening speed;
- start, turn, underwater, and breakout consistency;
- pace maintenance under rising fatigue;
- event-specific breathing and rhythm;
- strong final segment without technical collapse.

Programming guidance:

- combine fresh speed, broken race segments, pace-repeatability blocks, and aerobic support across the week;
- distinguish speed production from speed maintenance;
- verify target pace at segment checkpoints;
- give enough recovery to make race-pace work truly specific.

# 200 events

Common priorities:

- event-specific rhythm and distribution;
- efficient first half without passive swimming;
- controlled middle segments;
- ability to preserve mechanics and tempo late;
- turns and transitions across multiple fatigue states.

Programming guidance:

- use 25, 50, 75, and 100 segments for different questions;
- pair target pace with a stroke-specific quality measure;
- use broken or descending structures to teach shape;
- compare against the swimmer's own validated plan, not a universal split percentage.

# 400/500 events

Common priorities:

- sustainable early pace;
- stable middle rhythm;
- controlled response to field or plan changes;
- turn efficiency and counting discipline;
- finishing change without losing line.

Programming guidance:

- use pace bands derived from verified performance;
- accumulate meaningful work near the target while protecting form;
- include pace-awareness tasks without constant clock dependence;
- train turns as repeated performance contributors.

# 800/1000 and 1500/1650 events

Common priorities:

- durable speed and economical mechanics;
- repeatable turns, counting, and pacing;
- fueling/hydration planning when relevant and handled by appropriate staff;
- ability to change pace late;
- emotional control during long events.

Programming guidance:

- combine extensive aerobic work, protocol-derived sustainable pace, race-pace blocks, and controlled speed;
- avoid assuming that more weekly distance automatically produces better outcomes;
- monitor the response to load and preserve technique;
- use long swims only when they answer a specific question.

# Individual medley

Train:

- each stroke's legal and technical quality;
- transitions and turn rules;
- stroke-specific pacing rather than equal effort labels alone;
- the athlete's strongest and weakest segments;
- late freestyle execution after mixed-stroke load.

Use broken IM, transition-focused work, reverse IM, stroke-specific target segments, and full-IM rehearsals selectively. Do not let weak-stroke volume accumulate with poor mechanics merely to complete the sequence.

# Freestyle programming

Vary breathing, stroke-rate, distance-per-stroke, kick demand, and turn focus to match the objective. Do not reward artificially low stroke count at the expense of speed.

# Backstroke programming

Include orientation, flag/turn consistency, legal underwater practice, body-line and rotation work, and finish awareness. Track breakout performance and approach consistency.

# Breaststroke programming

Protect rhythm and legal execution. Use short technical repetitions when timing degrades. Separate kick-power work, pullout quality, cycle control, and full-stroke race segments where useful.

# Butterfly programming

Preserve whole-body rhythm. Use repeat distances the athlete can complete with the intended pattern. Alternate full stroke with transfer drills or choice swimming when needed; avoid using repeated failed butterfly as toughness training.

# Course effects

- SCY includes more turns per race distance and should emphasize repeated wall performance.
- SCM also increases turn frequency compared with LCM while preserving metric distances.
- LCM places longer uninterrupted demand on free-swimming rhythm and makes each start/turn fewer but still important.

Course conversions are planning estimates, not interchangeable performances or guaranteed entry times.

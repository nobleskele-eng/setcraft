# Use of cues

A cue is a testable teaching option, not a universal law. Match it to the observed problem, ask for one change, and verify the result. Do not infer a flaw from race time alone.

# Freestyle observations

Observe:

- head and trunk stability relative to breathing;
- hand entry relative to the swimmer's shoulder line;
- ability to hold water while the body travels forward;
- stroke-rate and distance-per-stroke interaction;
- kick timing and whether breathing changes body alignment;
- approach speed, wall distance, foot placement, and breakout after turns.

Possible cues:

- "Keep the line moving forward through the breath."
- "Anchor, then move the body past the hand."
- "Finish the turn speed into the wall, not at the flags."

Checks:

- compare non-breathing and breathing lengths;
- count strokes only when speed is also recorded;
- use side and front video before making a path claim.

# Backstroke observations

Observe:

- stable head and connected trunk rotation;
- hand entry, catch direction, and whether rotation timing supports pressure;
- kick continuity through rotation and breathing;
- flag count, turn initiation, legal execution, and underwater transition;
- finish awareness and legal wall contact.

Possible cues:

- "Rotate the body as one unit; keep the head quiet."
- "Carry speed under the flags and commit to the counted approach."
- "Break out into the first connected stroke, not a rushed recovery."

Do not prescribe a universal fixed flag count without confirming speed, pool, and athlete.

# Breaststroke observations

Observe:

- legal simultaneous movement and sequence;
- line after kick and pullout transitions;
- timing between propulsion and low-drag position;
- whether the kick recovers compactly relative to the athlete's mobility and skill;
- approach, open turn, push-off line, pullout, and breakout distance.

Possible cues:

- "Finish the kick into a narrow line."
- "Let the body travel before starting the next cycle."
- "Touch, turn, and leave in one clean sequence."

Knee pain or medical concerns are not technique problems for AI to solve. Treat entered restrictions as constraints and follow the real-world health process.

# Butterfly observations

Observe:

- whole-body rhythm rather than isolated arm force;
- entry width, catch connection, and recovery clearance;
- timing of kicks relative to entry and finish;
- breath timing and whether it disrupts forward travel;
- ability to preserve rhythm as speed changes;
- open-turn approach and underwater-to-surface transition.

Possible cues:

- "Send the chest forward, not straight down."
- "Keep the rhythm through the breath."
- "Use the breakout to join the first stroke, not fight it."

When rhythm collapses, shorten the repeat or increase recovery before repeating poor mechanics.

# Starts

Segment the start into setup, block phase, flight, entry, underwater, and breakout. Observe the transition between phases. Faster reaction alone does not guarantee a faster 15 m time. Start setup is individual; compare repeatable trials with the same timing definition.

Useful measures:

- block time if available;
- distance or position at entry;
- time to 5, 10, or 15 m with a fixed definition;
- underwater speed or time;
- breakout distance and first-cycle speed;
- trial-to-trial consistency.

# Turns

Segment a turn with clearly named boundaries, for example 5 m in to 5 m out. Observe approach velocity, wall contact or rotation, foot placement, push-off, underwater, breakout, and first-cycle connection. The best intervention targets the phase limiting the whole segment.

# Underwaters

Longer is not automatically faster. Compare underwater velocity with the swimmer's surface velocity and watch the transition. Use legal distance limits and event rules from the current governing document. Never recommend dangerous breath-holding contests or unsupervised hypoxic work.

# Drill selection test

A drill belongs in the plan only if the coach can answer:

1. Which observed behavior does it target?
2. What must transfer into swimming?
3. How will transfer be measured?
4. What error might the drill exaggerate?
5. When will the coach remove or progress it?

# Strategy is a tested hypothesis

A race plan should specify controllable actions, locked target checkpoints, a primary risk, and a validation session. It is not a prediction or guarantee.

# General pacing rules

- Use the split plan supplied by LaneLab; do not recalculate it in prose.
- Match comparisons by event and course.
- Separate start, turns, free swimming, and finish where data exists.
- Use no more than three cues during the race.
- Build an adjustment cue for the most likely failure mode.
- Validate the plan under coach supervision before the target meet.

# 50 strategy

Controllable priorities:

- stable start setup;
- committed entry and underwater transition;
- first-cycle connection;
- event-specific breath decision practiced safely;
- finish through the wall.

Primary risks:

- overreaching the start and losing entry line;
- staying underwater after speed has fallen below surface speed;
- changing the stroke under pressure;
- mistiming the finish.

Validation examples:

- repeated fixed-distance start trials with full recovery;
- flying speed segment plus finish;
- complete race rehearsal only when appropriate in the program.

# 100 strategy

Controllable priorities:

- first segment fast but organized;
- hold the planned rhythm through the first turn or transition;
- protect third-quarter mechanics;
- commit to the final segment and finish.

Primary risks:

- opening faster than the validated plan;
- treating the second segment as passive recovery;
- losing line or turn distance under fatigue;
- adding an untested breathing change.

Validation examples:

- broken 100 at locked checkpoints;
- 25s or 50s at target pace with adequate recovery and success count;
- start plus first race segment; turn plus closing segment.

# 200 strategy

Controllable priorities:

- establish event rhythm without forcing the first segment;
- hold the middle plan and avoid an unintentional third-segment fade;
- preserve stroke mechanics and turn execution;
- build the final segment from the validated point.

Primary risks:

- passive opening that creates an impossible close;
- first segment too costly for the athlete's tested profile;
- losing rhythm when effort increases;
- treating all four 50s as identical across strokes.

Validation examples:

- 4 x race segments at locked checkpoints with planned recovery;
- 100 plus 2 x 50 or 75/50/75 broken structures;
- technique metric paired with target pace.

# 400/500 strategy

Controllable priorities:

- settle quickly into the verified sustainable pace;
- keep middle splits inside a narrow band;
- execute every turn with the same approach plan;
- make the planned pace change before the finish becomes desperate.

Primary risks:

- adrenaline-driven first segment;
- mid-race drift hidden by average pace;
- waiting too late for a change;
- chasing another swimmer outside the plan.

# 800/1000 and 1500/1650 strategy

Controllable priorities:

- disciplined opening;
- repeatable middle rhythm and wall count;
- planned response points;
- final build in stages;
- awareness of field without abandoning the validated plan.

Use rolling split groups and cue points. Do not overload the athlete with dozens of exact numbers during the race.

# IM strategy

- Plan by stroke and transition, not one global effort number.
- Protect legal turns and the transition into each stroke.
- Avoid overspending on the strongest stroke if it compromises later segments.
- Define how the athlete exits the weakest stroke.
- Preserve a specific freestyle close.

# Stroke-specific cues

Freestyle: connect breathing plan to line and tempo.

Backstroke: maintain orientation, flag plan, turn commitment, and breakout connection.

Breaststroke: protect rhythm, line, legal pullout, and turn timing; do not chase stroke rate alone.

Butterfly: protect whole-body rhythm and breath timing; a shorter controlled cycle can be preferable to forced length or rushed rate depending on the athlete.

Source anchor: PMID 29560605. Pacing patterns vary by event, distance, stroke, and performance level; no universal split percentage should be imposed.
